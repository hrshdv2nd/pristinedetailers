## Seasonal Context

Late August in Melbourne is the best calendar window for paint correction bookings. Winter creates quiet, compounding paint damage — and the pre-spring moment is when a paint-conscious car owner starts noticing it.

**The pre-spring reveal problem.** Melbourne's spring returns in September. That's when the good-weather drives come back: weekend runs to the Mornington Peninsula, the car comes out from the garage more often, and direct sunlight is no longer at the low winter angle that hides surface damage. A Porsche or BMW that looked fine under overcast August skies will show every swirl mark, oxidation ring, and bird-etch scratch under October sun at 45 degrees. The owner who left it alone all winter will see it. The one who booked paint correction in August won't. This is the moment to intervene — before the reveal.

**The auto wash accumulation.** Petrol station auto washes use rotating brushes that drag grit across clear coat. Owners who've been running their prestige car through one every fortnight all winter have put a season's worth of micro-scratches into their paint — all of them nearly invisible in shade, all of them visible under direct sun or under the beam of their own headlights at night. Most owners don't know this is happening because the damage is cumulative and slow. The ad that names it specifically — BMW, Porsche, winter, auto wash — earns recognition. Recognition earns trust. Trust earns the click.

**The pre-coating gate.** A meaningful portion of paint correction customers arrive through a research path: they looked up ceramic coating, found out that paint correction is required before any coating application (because ceramic bonds permanently to whatever is already on the clear coat — including swirls), and now need correction done first. These are high-intent, product-aware buyers. Their conversion path is different: they're not discovering that paint correction exists; they're looking for someone to trust with the prep work before a $750+ investment. They should be in a separate ad set with a different variant.

**Three distinct cold audiences for this week:**
1. **Pre-spring refresh** (Toorak, South Yarra, Hawthorn, Kew — Inner East): Winter-damage, seasonal angle. Prestige vehicle owners who care but haven't acted yet. Visual-first creative showing swirl marks under direct light.
2. **Auto wash damage** (Broad Inner East + Bayside): Behaviour-based call-out. People who recognise themselves in the "I've been using the auto wash" description. Slightly broader postcode targeting.
3. **Pre-coating audience** (Warm, product-aware via pixel retargeting of `/services/ceramic-coating` or `/services/ppf` visitors): The correction-before-coating angle. More text-heavy. Higher intent. Lower volume but significantly higher close rate.

## Ad Variants

### Variant 1 — Pre-Spring Reveal (Angle: Winter left marks. Fix them before spring exposes them.)

**Primary text (115 chars):**
> Winter does quiet damage to your paint. Swirls, oxidation, bird etch — invisible until spring sun hits. Fix it now.

**Headline (25 chars):**
> Paint Correction. Mobile.

**Description (23 chars):**
> From $300. No Workshop.

**Why this works:** "Winter does quiet damage" is the precise truth: the damage is real, ongoing, and quiet — not dramatic or sudden. It's the kind of problem that feels abstract until someone names it. "Swirls, oxidation, bird etch" gives it texture — these are three distinct damage types with different causes, and listing all three makes the threat feel comprehensive without being alarmist. "Invisible until spring sun hits" is the temporal hook: it converts August into a decision moment, not an abstract one. A car owner who has felt the anxiety of not knowing what their paint looks like under real sun reads this and feels it. "Fix it now" is a minimal CTA — no discount, no urgency theatre, just a direct instruction to act while the window is right.

The headline "Paint Correction. Mobile." does two jobs in four words: names the service and removes the workshop friction. The description "From $300. No Workshop." closes both remaining objections — price transparency and logistics — in five words. Total copy across all three fields: 163 chars. No wasted character.

**Creative direction:**
- Hero static (Facebook Feed): Split-light photograph — half the bonnet in deep shade (paint appears fine, dark, even), the other half in direct late-afternoon sun (swirl marks visible as circular hazing). No graphic overlay. No text on image. Let the photography do the work — the viewer's eye naturally drifts to the damaged half. The residential context (Toorak double garage, brick wall in background) establishes that this car lives where the target audience lives. Car make: silver or white BMW 5 Series or Mercedes E-Class — aspirationally familiar but not exotic enough to feel out of reach.
- Instagram Feed: Tight close-up of clear coat showing swirl halo under raking sunlight. No car visible — just the surface, at macro level. Caption: "This is what winter + auto washes do. This is what one session of paint correction undoes." Below the image: Headline + description as per spec.
- Reels/Stories: 10-second split-screen timelapse — machine polisher working one section of a dark bonnet, before/after wipe-down with quick detailer, then light passes over the freshly corrected section. No voiceover. Text overlay (bottom third): "One Melbourne winter. One machine polish. This is what changed." CTA: "Book paint correction."

**Audience:** Cold. Inner East postcodes (Toorak 3142, South Yarra 3141, Hawthorn 3122, Kew 3101, Malvern 3144). Interests: BMW, Porsche, Mercedes-Benz brand pages. Demographics: 28–55, homeowners (proxy for garage access). Exclude: existing Pristine customers last 24 months; active members.

### Variant 2 — Auto Wash Damage (Angle: The thing you've been doing all winter is wrecking your clear coat.)

**Primary text (111 chars):**
> Every auto wash puts swirl marks on your clear coat. Washing your BMW or Porsche that way all winter, it shows.

**Headline (27 chars):**
> Swirl Removal. Come To You.

**Description (25 chars):**
> 4.9★ · $300. No Drop-Off.

**Why this works:** "Every auto wash" is a universal behaviour — nearly every prestige car owner has used one at some point, especially through winter when hand-washing in the cold is genuinely unpleasant. This copy works because it names a recognisable action, not an abstract risk. "Puts swirl marks on your clear coat" is technically accurate and gently educational — the reader learns something while being implicated. The pivot: "Washing your BMW or Porsche that way all winter, it shows." Landing on the word "shows" does two things — it implies that the damage is already visible (which creates an urgency to look), and it delivers a mild social consequence (other people can see it too). No accusation. Just cause and effect.

The headline "Swirl Removal. Come To You." is specific about the service outcome (swirl removal, not just "detailing") and restates the mobile differentiator in three words. Description pairs the aggregate trust proof with price and the anti-friction message. The combination — swirl marks named, service outcome named, price given, friction removed — covers the full decision checklist in 163 chars.

**Creative direction:**
- Static (Facebook + Instagram Feed): Overhead shot of a dark-coloured bonnet (navy or graphite) under fluorescent lighting or direct sun — swirl haze clearly visible as a circular web pattern. Split frame: left half untouched, right half freshly corrected (high gloss, depth visible). Caption: "Left: 6 months of auto wash. Right: one session of paint correction. Same car." Raw photography, consistent lighting, consistent angle. No stock images. This should be shot on a real Melbourne customer's car.
- Story/Reels format: Point-of-view video — a machine polisher running across a graphite BMW 3 Series bonnet in a residential driveway (established suburb visible in background). Cut every 5 seconds between polisher running and wiped section showing gloss. Total 12 seconds. No music. Text at bottom: "Auto washes leave swirl marks every time. This removes them." CTA button: "Get paint correction."

**Audience:** Cold, slightly broader. Postcode targeting expanded to include Bayside (Brighton 3186, Brighton East 3187, Hampton 3188, Sandringham 3191, Beaumaris 3193) in addition to Inner East. Interests: BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Audi, Lexus, Tesla (luxury vehicle segment). Behaviour overlay: frequent travellers and high-income households. This variant is designed for breadth — the auto wash behaviour is near-universal, not brand-specific.

**Note on car naming:** BMW and Porsche are named in the primary text because they are the most recognised prestige brands in the target postcode cluster. Do not substitute generic phrasing ("luxury car", "prestige vehicle") — brand-name specificity generates the recognition response that triggers the scroll-stop. If running per interest group, swap the brand name to match the interest: "your Audi or Range Rover" for the Audi/Land Rover interest group.

### Variant 3 — Pre-Coating Gate (Angle: Ceramic is permanent. What it seals in stays sealed in. Correct first.)

**Primary text (118 chars):**
> Ceramic coating locks in whatever's already on your paint — including every swirl and scratch. Correction comes first.

**Headline (27 chars):**
> Correct First. Coat Second.

**Description (26 chars):**
> We Come To You. From $300.

**Why this works:** This variant is written for a specific, product-aware buyer: someone who has already researched ceramic coating and knows they need paint correction first, but hasn't yet booked anyone to do it. "Ceramic coating locks in whatever's already on your paint" is the exact argument they've already read — hearing it stated plainly, without sales pressure, confirms that this brand understands the product correctly. "Including every swirl and scratch" puts the consequence in concrete terms. "Correction comes first" is the logical conclusion delivered without padding. The reader who is ready for this doesn't need a metaphor or a narrative — they need a clear statement from a brand that knows what they're doing.

The headline "Correct First. Coat Second." mirrors how a certified detailer would talk to a client about the process — instructive, sequential, expert. It presupposes that the viewer is considering a coating, which is the signal that separates this variant's audience from the general swirl-removal buyer. "We Come To You. From $300." handles the two remaining questions: logistics and price.

**Creative direction:**
- Static (Facebook): Side-by-side diagram — not photography. Left panel: ceramic coating cross-section showing nano-ceramic bonding on top of swirled clear coat (swirls visible in the layer below the ceramic). Right panel: paint correction step shown with polisher, then ceramic applied to a flat, smooth clear coat. Label: "What you get when you skip correction. What you get when you don't." Clean sans-serif. White background. This is an educational ad — it should look authoritative, not promotional. A buyer who has done their research will trust the brand that uses correct technical diagrams over the brand that uses a glamour shot of a shiny car.
- Static (Instagram Feed): Close-up of gloss black paint surface post-correction — extreme depth and reflection visible, almost mirror-like. Overlay (small, corner text): "Before we coat: correction. This is the result." Below image: headline + description. This is the aspirational version of the same argument — the educational variant runs on Facebook where the demographic (35–55) responds to information; the aspirational variant runs on Instagram where the same demographic responds to craft.
- Instagram Reels (15 sec): Machine polisher working across a section of dark paint, then a quick wipe-down revealing the corrected gloss, then an immediate ceramic application across the same section. Text overlay: "Step 1: Correct. Step 2: Coat. We do both. We come to you." No voiceover. The process is the proof.

**Audience:** Warm retargeting of pixel audiences who visited `/services/ceramic-coating` or `/services/ppf` in the last 30 days but did not reach `/booking`. This is the audience most likely to convert — they have done the product research and have already self-identified as buyers. Paint correction is the decision gate they're sitting at. This variant does not run as a cold audience ad; the language is too specific for buyers who haven't yet reached the coating consideration stage. Budget allocation: 20–25% of total paint correction budget.

## Creative Direction Notes

**Image/video guidance for the week:**
- **Hero static (Facebook Feed):** Certified technician operating a dual-action polisher on a dark-coloured bonnet — graphite or navy BMW, Mercedes, or Porsche in a Toorak or Hawthorn residential driveway. Morning light from the left (golden hour direction), technician in branded Pristine workwear. The paint surface in the foreground should show the circular motion haze before correction and the mirror-like gloss in the corrected section beside the polisher. The residential setting — double garage, established garden, red brick — establishes suburb identity immediately. This is not a studio. This is their neighbourhood.
- **Before/after Instagram Feed:** Tight, flat macro shot of a dark bonnet. Left frame: natural overhead light, swirl haze visible as a cobweb pattern. Right frame: raking side light, freshly corrected surface showing mirror-depth gloss. Consistent framing and lighting temperature. No graphic treatment — raw photography, same car, same panel. Caption: "One machine polish. Same car. Same bonnet. Melbourne." No exclamation marks. No superlatives.
- **Reels/Stories (15–20 sec):** Machine polisher working across a graphite BMW 3 Series bonnet in real time. No fast cuts — show the actual work, the detail, the patience. Mid-way wipe-down to reveal the corrected section. Cut to the final result: overhead sun on mirror-finish paint, technician stepping back. No voiceover. Text overlay (lower third, white sans-serif): "Paint correction. Mobile. From $300." Final 3 seconds: Pristine wordmark + "Book at pristinedetailers.com.au" — no animation, clean hold.
- **Avoid:** Stock photography of any kind; any American or British vehicle or driveway; stylised renders or 3D effects; close-ups of hands that don't read as professional; any before/after image that looks staged or edited rather than photographed from a real correction job

**Format priority for Paint Correction:**
1. Instagram Feed — V1 (before/after, seasonal) and V2 (auto wash damage close-up); visual-first format; the before/after impact is strongest on Instagram's square or 4:5 crop
2. Facebook Feed — V3 (pre-coating gate); wider text format suits the educational, diagram-style approach; older demographic (35–55) on Facebook responds to informational copy
3. Instagram Reels — machine polish timelapse; native format, high organic engagement; the craft and patience on display in a real polishing video outperforms any testimonial for a buyer anxious about who they're trusting
4. Stories (retargeting only) — single static image with headline + CTA; frequency-capped at 2× per week; do not use for cold prospecting this week

**Copy test plan (weeks 1–2):**
- Cold audience: V1 (pre-spring seasonal) vs V2 (auto wash damage) across Inner East — measure CTR and cost-per-click-to-paint-correction-page; do not mix into one ad set; clear winner should be identified after 1,000 impressions per ad set
- V3 (pre-coating gate) runs only against warm pixel audience; compare lead quality (quote request rate) against the cold variants, not CTR — intent is different, benchmark is different
- Retargeting budget: 15–20% of total paint correction budget this week; paint correction at $300 has a shorter consideration window than PPF at $3,000, so retargeting should convert faster
- Kill any cold variant at <0.8% CTR after 1,000 impressions (paint correction at $300 has a higher benchmark than PPF — lower price = broader accessible audience = higher expected CTR); consolidate budget to winner after 7 days
